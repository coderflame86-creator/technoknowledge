Teachers spend hours every week on work outside the classroom: lesson plans, worksheets, quizzes, slides and feedback. AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Claude and Canva AI can do the first draft of much of that work in minutes. The teacher’s job becomes reviewing, improving and teaching.

This post explains how teachers can start, and what to watch out for.

## What can AI help teachers with?

- **Lesson planning:** clear, structured lesson plans and lecture notes for any topic
- **Tests and assessments:** quizzes, assignments, rubrics and faster, consistent feedback
- **Presentations:** classroom slide decks created from a topic or chapter
- **Textbook to teaching kit:** turning a textbook chapter into a summary, slides and an assessment
- **Video and voice:** short video and audio content for revision

## Start with a good prompt

The quality of what AI gives you depends on what you ask. A weak prompt gets a weak answer. Compare:

- **Weak:** “Make a quiz on photosynthesis.”
- **Better:** “Make a 10-question quiz on photosynthesis for Grade 6, with answers. Use simple English. Include 6 multiple-choice and 4 short-answer questions.”

A good prompt says **who** it is for (grade and level), **what** you want (format and length), and **any rules** (language, difficulty, style).

## Always check the output

AI can be confidently wrong. Before you use anything in class:

- Check every fact and answer yourself
- Make sure it matches your syllabus and textbook
- Look for bias: does it represent people fairly?
- Edit it into your own voice and your students’ level

AI gives you a draft. You are still the teacher.

## Use your own textbooks

The most useful results come when you give AI your own material: paste in a textbook passage and ask for a summary, key vocabulary, a worksheet and a quiz based only on that text. This keeps the content aligned with what your students are actually studying.

## Protect academic integrity

Students use AI too. Talk openly with them about when AI is allowed and when it is not, and design some tasks that need students’ own thinking, such as explaining their reasoning aloud or applying an idea to their own life.
